<p class="page-deck"><em>How participant mental models shaped the redesigned app structure</em></p>

The information architecture work used two studies in sequence. A **closed card sort**
first showed how participants conceptually grouped app functions. A **first-choice
tree test** then challenged selected placement decisions in a navigation context.

The studies were used as design evidence rather than as a mechanical voting
system. Where participant expectations remained divided, the redesign also
considered navigation simplicity, competitive research, and product-design
judgment.

## 1. What mental model did the card sort reveal?

<StatisticalPlot src={chart0} alt="Most functions had a recognizable conceptual home" />

The first study established a useful baseline. **Account-related functions**
formed a coherent cluster, and core device-management functions were also
placed consistently. Overall, **13 of 17 cards** reached
at least 70% agreement.

The uncertainty was not spread evenly across the IA. It was concentrated around
a specific boundary: **Device Settings versus Customize**.

## 2. Where did the first structure become unstable?

<StatisticalPlot src={chart1} alt="The weak point was the boundary between settings and customization" />

Media controls, tap/vibration settings, notifications, and call controls did
not point cleanly to one category. This was the important card-sort finding:
the broad structure worked, but **Device Settings and Customize were competing
mental models for the same family of controls**.

That uncertainty became a primary target for the follow-up tree test.

## 3. Did the navigation test resolve those boundaries?

<StatisticalPlot src={chart2} alt="The follow-up clarified some placements and challenged others" />

The tree test did not simply confirm the study plan. **Media Controls** became
clearly associated with Customize, and **Connected Apps** continued to lean
toward Device Settings. **Tap & Vibration Preferences** remained evenly divided.
**Vibration Alerts** leaned toward Device Settings rather than the proposed
Customize destination.

The largest change was **Help & Support**: the card sort had grouped it with
My Account, but in navigation most participants began under Device Settings.

The two methods answered different questions. The card sort asked,
**“What belongs together?”** The tree test asked, **“Where would you start
looking?”** Their differences therefore became useful design evidence rather
than something that needed to be averaged away.

Three patterns mattered most:

**Some expectations strengthened.** Media Controls moved from a narrow
Customize preference in the card sort to a much clearer Customize first choice
in the tree test.

**Some ambiguity persisted.** Tap and vibration controls remained divided
between Device Settings and Customize.

**Some conceptual grouping did not predict navigation.** Help & Support is the
clearest example: participants grouped it with My Account in the card sort but
looked for it under Device Settings in the tree.

The final redesign used **Home, Insights, Settings, and Account** as its four
major destinations.

The most important synthesis was **Settings**. Instead of preserving
Device Settings and Customize as competing top-level destinations, the redesign
placed configuration functions within one broader Settings area and organized
them into clearer subsections. This absorbed the recurring ambiguity around
tap/vibration, notifications, media controls, connections, and related controls.

At the same time, the strongest card-sort patterns were preserved. **Account**
remained distinct, while device-specific management received a direct
device-status entry point from Home.

The research therefore did not produce a final sitemap by majority vote.
It identified **stable mental models, unstable boundaries, and navigation
expectations**. The redesign used those signals—alongside competitive research,
simplification goals, and design judgment—to decide where separation was useful
and where consolidation was better.

### Method note

The closed card sort included **49 participants** placing
**17 cards** into three predefined categories. Agreement is the share
placing a card in its most-selected category.

The first-choice tree test included **49 participants** and focused on
five selected navigation decisions. It records only the top-level branch chosen
first. The gold outlines in the tree-test visualization represent the
hypothesized destination used in the study design.

The two methods are interpreted together but not treated as interchangeable:
card sorting measures conceptual grouping, while first choice measures initial
navigation expectation.
